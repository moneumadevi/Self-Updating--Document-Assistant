TF-IDF and Document Retrieval - Study Notes

What is TF-IDF?

TF-IDF stands for Term Frequency - Inverse Document Frequency. It is a numerical
method that converts text into numbers so a computer can compare documents. Every
word gets a weight. A word that appears often in one document but rarely in the
other documents gets a high weight, because it is a good clue for that document.

Term Frequency (TF) measures how often a word appears in a document. Inverse
Document Frequency (IDF) measures how rare the word is across all documents. The
final weight is TF multiplied by IDF. Common words like "the" and "is" appear in
almost every document, so their IDF is low and they are usually removed using a
stop-word list.

What is a vector?

After TF-IDF, every chunk becomes a long list of numbers, called a vector. The list
has one position for every word in the vocabulary. Most positions are zero because a
single passage uses only a small part of the vocabulary, so the data is stored as a
sparse matrix to save memory.

What is cosine similarity?

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors. If the angle is small the
similarity is close to 1, which means the passage talks about the same words as the
question. If the vectors share no words the similarity is 0. Cosine similarity is
popular for text because it ignores the length of the document: a long document and
a short document about the same topic still score high.

How does question answering work with TF-IDF?

1. The documents are split into small chunks of about 120 words.
2. The TF-IDF vectorizer is fit on all chunks, so it learns the vocabulary and the
   IDF weights.
3. Each chunk is transformed into a vector and stored in a matrix.
4. The user's question is transformed with the same vectorizer.
5. Cosine similarity is computed between the question vector and every chunk vector.
6. The highest scoring chunks are returned as the answer, together with the source
   file name.

Is TF-IDF machine learning?

TF-IDF is a classical natural language processing technique. The vectorizer is fit
on the corpus, which means it learns statistics such as document frequency from your
data. It is not a neural network, it does not need a GPU, and it does not need a
download of a large pre-trained model. Training happens in milliseconds and the
result is fully explainable.

Why chunks instead of whole documents?

A whole document may cover ten topics, so its vector becomes an average and matches
everything weakly. A small chunk covers one idea, so the similarity score becomes
sharper and the quoted answer is short and readable.

Limitations of TF-IDF

TF-IDF matches words, not meaning. If the question says "car" and the document says
"automobile", the score may be zero. Synonyms, spelling mistakes and very long
questions can reduce accuracy. Vector embeddings from a neural model solve this, but
they need a model download and more computing power.

Why we still use it for a student project

TF-IDF is fast, free, offline, deterministic and easy to explain in a viva. You can
show the exact weights, the cosine score and the matched passage, and the result is
reproducible on any laptop.
